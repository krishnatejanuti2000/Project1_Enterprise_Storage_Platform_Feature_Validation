# RAID 10

## 1. Overview

RAID 10 combines:

- Mirroring
- Striping

Its fundamental design is:

```text
RAID 1
    ↓
Mirroring
    +
RAID 0
    ↓
Striping
    ↓
RAID 10
```

The purpose of RAID 10 is to provide:

```text
High I/O performance
        +
Data redundancy
```

Unlike RAID 5 and RAID 6, RAID 10 does not use parity.

```text
No P parity
No Q parity
No XOR parity calculation
No Reed–Solomon calculation
```

Instead, redundancy comes from maintaining multiple copies of
the data.

---

# 2. Why RAID 10 Exists

RAID 0 provides:

```text
Striping
+
Parallel I/O
+
Performance
```

but has no redundancy.

RAID 1 provides:

```text
Mirroring
+
Redundancy
```

but does not provide the same striped multi-mirror-group access
pattern as RAID 10.

RAID 10 combines the two concepts:

```text
Mirroring
    +
Striping
```

The resulting architecture provides strong I/O performance while
maintaining redundant copies of the data.

---

# 3. RAID 10 Architecture

For the classic four-member RAID 10 arrangement:

```text
D1 ↔ D2
D3 ↔ D4
```

The first pair forms one mirror set.

The second pair forms another mirror set.

Data is then striped across the mirror sets.

Conceptually:

```text
             RAID 10
                |
       ┌────────┴────────┐
       ↓                 ↓
   Mirror Pair 1     Mirror Pair 2
      D1 ↔ D2           D3 ↔ D4
       \                 /
        \               /
         ── striping ───
```

The classic four-member layout can be visualized as:

```text
D1    D2    D3    D4
 A     A     B     B
 C     C     D     D
 E     E     F     F
 G     G     H     H
```

Each logical data block has two copies.

---

# 4. RAID 10 Data Redundancy

Suppose:

```text
A
```

is stored on the first mirror set.

It exists as:

```text
D1 → A
D2 → A
```

Therefore:

```text
D1 failure
    ↓
D2 still contains A
```

Similarly:

```text
D3 → B
D4 → B
```

If D3 fails:

```text
D4 still contains B
```

The redundancy mechanism is therefore:

```text
Duplicate data copies
```

rather than:

```text
Parity
```

---

# 5. No Parity in RAID 10

RAID 10 does not calculate:

```text
P
Q
XOR
Reed–Solomon
Galois Field arithmetic
```

for normal RAID redundancy.

Instead:

```text
New data
    ↓
Multiple copies
```

This makes the redundancy mechanism conceptually simpler than RAID
5/6 parity calculations.

---

# 6. RAID 10 Read Behavior

For a healthy mirror set:

```text
D1 ↔ D2
```

both members contain the same data.

A read request can therefore be satisfied from an available mirror
member.

Conceptually:

```text
Host
 ↓
RAID 10
 ↓
D1 or D2
 ↓
Requested data
```

Multiple mirror members can participate in serving read requests,
depending on workload and implementation.

RAID 10 does not need a parity calculation for a normal healthy read.

---

# 7. RAID 10 Write Behavior

When new data is written to a mirror set:

```text
New Data
   ↓
D1
+
D2
```

Both mirror copies must be maintained.

Example:

```text
Write X

D1 → X
D2 → X
```

There is no parity update.

Therefore the logical write requirement is:

```text
Update mirror copy 1
+
Update mirror copy 2
```

---

# 8. RAID 10 Small Random I/O

RAID 10 avoids the parity-maintenance calculations associated with
RAID 5 and RAID 6.

Conceptually:

```text
RAID 10 write:

New Data
   ↓
Mirror
 ↓    ↓
D1    D2
```

Compared with parity RAID:

```text
RAID 6:

New Data
   ↓
Maintain P
+
Maintain Q
```

This is one reason RAID 10 is attractive for workloads that require
strong random I/O performance.

---

# 9. RAID 10 Single-Member Failure

Healthy:

```text
D1 ↔ D2
D3 ↔ D4
```

Suppose:

```text
D1 → FAILED
D2 → HEALTHY
D3 → HEALTHY
D4 → HEALTHY
```

D1's mirror copy still exists on D2.

Therefore:

```text
D1 failure
    ↓
D2 supplies the surviving copy
    ↓
Data remains accessible
```

The RAID system does not need parity reconstruction.

---

# 10. RAID 10 Two-Member Failure

Two-member failure behavior depends on which members fail.

This is one of the most important RAID 10 concepts.

---

## 10.1 Two Failures in Different Mirror Sets

Example:

```text
D1 → FAILED
D3 → FAILED

D2 → HEALTHY
D4 → HEALTHY
```

Mirror sets:

```text
D1 ↔ D2
     ↑
    D2 survives

D3 ↔ D4
     ↑
    D4 survives
```

Every mirror set still has one surviving member.

Therefore the array can continue operating.

```text
D1 ❌    D2 ✅
D3 ❌    D4 ✅
```

---

## 10.2 Two Failures in the Same Mirror Set

Example:

```text
D1 → FAILED
D2 → FAILED
```

Then:

```text
D1 ❌ ↔ D2 ❌
```

The entire mirror set is lost.

The data stored only in that mirror set has no surviving copy.

RAID 10 has no parity mechanism to reconstruct it.

Therefore:

```text
Entire mirror set lost
        ↓
Data unavailable
```

---

# 11. RAID 10 Fault-Tolerance Rule

The most important RAID 10 rule is:

> Every mirror set must retain at least one surviving member.

Therefore:

```text
1 failure
    ↓
Normally survivable

2 failures in different mirror sets
    ↓
Survivable

2 failures in the same mirror set
    ↓
Not survivable
```

This means RAID 10 does not have a simple fixed statement of:

```text
"RAID 10 always tolerates two drive failures."
```

Instead, fault tolerance depends on the failure pattern.

---

# 12. RAID 10 Reconstruction

RAID 10 reconstruction is fundamentally a copy operation.

Suppose:

```text
D1 → FAILED
D2 → HEALTHY
```

D2 already contains the missing data.

Therefore:

```text
D2
 ↓
Read surviving mirror copy
 ↓
Replacement
```

No parity calculation is required.

---

# 13. RAID 10 Rebuild

When a replacement drive is inserted:

```text
D1 → FAILED
D2 → HEALTHY
D1' → Replacement
```

The system rebuilds the missing mirror copy:

```text
D2
 ↓
Read data
 ↓
Write identical data
 ↓
D1'
```

After completion:

```text
D1' ↔ D2
```

The mirror is restored.

---

# 14. Reconstruction vs Rebuild

These concepts remain separate.

## Reconstruction

```text
Calculate / obtain the missing information
```

For RAID 10, the information already exists on the surviving mirror.

## Rebuild

```text
Restore the missing mirror copy onto a replacement member.
```

Conceptually:

```text
Failed member
      ↓
Replacement member
      ↓
Copy surviving mirror
      ↓
Mirror restored
```

---

# 15. RAID 10 Rebuild Workload

Although RAID 10 does not need parity reconstruction, rebuild still
requires significant I/O.

During rebuild:

```text
Normal application I/O
        +
Read surviving mirror
        +
Write replacement
```

Therefore:

```text
Rebuild
    ↓
Additional storage workload
    ↓
Potential performance impact
```

---

# 16. RAID 10 Rebuild Risk

Consider:

```text
D1 → FAILED
D2 → HEALTHY

D3 → FAILED
D4 → HEALTHY
```

Both mirror sets are degraded:

```text
D1 ❌ ↔ D2 ✅
D3 ❌ ↔ D4 ✅
```

Suppose D1 is being rebuilt.

If D2 fails before the first mirror set is restored:

```text
D1 ❌
D2 ❌
```

then:

```text
D1 ❌ ↔ D2 ❌
```

The first mirror set is completely lost.

Therefore:

> RAID 10 rebuild state and failure location are both operationally
> important.

---

# 17. RAID 10 Capacity

For equal-sized members:

```text
Usable Capacity ≈ (N / 2) × Member Size
```

RAID 10 provides approximately 50% usable capacity.

Example:

```text
4 × 1 TB
```

Raw:

```text
4 TB
```

Usable:

```text
2 TB
```

Another example:

```text
8 × 1 TB
```

Raw:

```text
8 TB
```

Usable:

```text
4 TB
```

The remaining capacity is consumed by mirrored copies.

---

# 18. Minimum RAID 10 Members

The normal minimum for a two-copy RAID 10 arrangement is:

```text
4 members
```

Conceptually:

```text
D1 ↔ D2
D3 ↔ D4
```

Two mirror sets are then striped together.

---

# 19. RAID 10 Performance Model

RAID 10 is designed to provide strong I/O performance through:

```text
Striping
+
Multiple physical devices
+
Multiple mirror copies
```

It avoids the parity-maintenance calculations of RAID 5/6.

This can make RAID 10 attractive for workloads where:

```text
High IOPS
Low write latency
Fast rebuild behavior
```

are important.

---

# 20. RAID 10 vs RAID 5

| Feature              | RAID 5                 | RAID 10                     |
| -------------------- | ---------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Redundancy           | XOR parity             | Mirroring                   |
| Striping             | Yes                    | Yes                         |
| Parity calculation   | Yes                    | No                          |
| Usable capacity      | ~N-1 members           | ~N/2 members                |
| Write path           | Parity maintenance     | Mirror writes               |
| Rebuild              | Parity reconstruction  | Mirror copy                 |
| Two-failure behavior | Normally not tolerated | Depends on mirror placement |

---

# 21. RAID 10 vs RAID 6

| Feature                 | RAID 6             | RAID 10                      |
| ----------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| Redundancy              | P + Q              | Mirroring                    |
| Striping                | Yes                | Yes                          |
| Parity                  | Yes                | No                           |
| Usable capacity         | `(N-2) × size`     | Approximately `(N/2) × size` |
| Two failures            | Normally tolerated | Depends on failure placement |
| Small-write parity cost | Higher             | No parity calculation        |
| Rebuild                 | P/Q reconstruction | Mirror copy                  |

---

# 22. RAID 10 Layouts in Linux `mdadm`

Linux software RAID 10 is more flexible than the simple
four-drive mirror-pair diagram.

`mdadm` supports RAID 10 layouts including:

```text
near
far
offset
```

The layout determines how mirrored copies are placed.

The classic two-copy layout is:

```text
near=2
```

The number indicates the number of copies, while the layout name
describes copy placement.

Therefore:

```text
near=2
```

means:

```text
2 copies
+
near placement
```

It does not mean:

```text
2 mirror pairs
```

---

# 23. RAID 10 `near` Layout

In a `near` layout, mirrored copies are placed near each other in
the logical layout.

Conceptually:

```text
A A
B B
C C
D D
```

The exact physical placement depends on the implementation and
selected RAID geometry.

The important idea is:

```text
near
    ↓
copies are placed relatively close together
```

---

# 24. RAID 10 `far` Layout

The `far` layout places mirrored copies farther apart in the layout.

The purpose is to distribute the copies differently across the
underlying media and can improve certain sequential I/O behaviors.

The fundamental property remains:

```text
Multiple copies
+
Striping
```

---

# 25. RAID 10 `offset` Layout

The `offset` layout uses an offset placement strategy for the second
copy.

Again:

```text
Multiple copies
+
Different physical/logical placement
```

The layout changes copy placement, not the basic redundancy model.

---

# 26. `mdadm` RAID 10 Creation

A basic RAID 10 creation command is:

```bash
sudo mdadm --create /dev/md10 \
    --level=10 \
    --raid-devices=4 \
    /dev/sdb /dev/sdc /dev/sdd /dev/sde
```

The important option is:

```text
--level=10
```

which selects RAID 10.

An explicit RAID 10 layout can also be configured using the
appropriate `--layout` option supported by the installed `mdadm`
version.

During the lab, the actual layout will be inspected rather than
assumed.

---

Important commands include:

```bash
cat /proc/mdstat
```

and:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md10
```

Important fields:

```text
RAID Level
Raid Devices
Total Devices
Layout
Chunk Size
State
Active Devices
Working Devices
Failed Devices
Spare Devices
```

---

# 28. RAID 10 Degraded-State Lifecycle

The normal lifecycle is:

```text
HEALTHY
   ↓
Member Failure
   ↓
DEGRADED
   ↓
Replacement
   ↓
REBUILD
   ↓
HEALTHY
```

During degraded operation:

```text
Data remains available
    if every mirror set retains
    at least one surviving member.
```

---

# 29. RAID 10 Hot Spare

A hot spare is a standby disk that can become a rebuild target.

Example:

```text
Active RAID 10:
D1 D2 D3 D4

Hot Spare:
D5
```

After a failure:

```text
Member failure
      ↓
Hot spare
      ↓
Rebuild
```

The hot spare does not change the fundamental RAID 10 redundancy
model.

It is a recovery resource.

---

# 30. RAID 10 Engineering Mental Model

```text
                    RAID 10
                       |
             ┌─────────┴─────────┐
             ↓                   ↓
        Mirroring             Striping
             ↓                   ↓
       Redundancy            Performance
             \                 /
              \               /
               RAID 10
```

Failure model:

```text
Every mirror set
must retain ≥ 1 surviving member
```

Rebuild model:

```text
Surviving mirror
      ↓
Read
      ↓
Replacement
      ↓
Write identical data
      ↓
Mirror restored
```

---

# 31. Key RAID 10 Points

1. RAID 10 combines mirroring and striping.
2. RAID 10 is commonly described as RAID 1+0.
3. RAID 10 provides redundancy through multiple copies.
4. RAID 10 does not use parity.
5. RAID 10 does not use Reed–Solomon coding.
6. RAID 10 does not require Galois Field arithmetic for normal
   redundancy.
7. RAID 10 normally requires at least four members.
8. Usable capacity is approximately 50% for equal-sized members.
9. One member failure is normally survivable.
10. Two failures are survivable when they occur in different mirror
    sets.
11. Losing both members of one mirror set causes loss of that data.
12. RAID 10 rebuilds by copying the surviving mirror.
13. RAID 10 does not need parity reconstruction for a normal
    single-member rebuild.
14. Rebuild still generates additional read/write workload.
15. Hot spares provide a rebuild target.
16. `mdadm` supports RAID 10 layouts such as near, far and offset.
17. `near=2` means two copies using the near layout.
18. Actual Linux layout should be verified with `mdadm --detail`.
19. RAID 10 is attractive for performance-sensitive workloads.
20. RAID 10 trades capacity efficiency for mirrored redundancy and
    performance.

---

# 32. Summary

The RAID 10 design can be summarized as:

```text
RAID 10
   ↓
Mirroring + Striping
   ↓
Multiple copies
   +
Parallel I/O
```

There is no:

```text
P
Q
XOR parity
Reed–Solomon
Galois Field
```

Instead:

```text
Data
 ↓
Copy 1
Copy 2
```

The most important failure rule is:

```text
Every mirror set
must retain at least one surviving member.
```

Therefore:

```text
One failure
    → normally survivable

Two failures in different mirror sets
    → survivable

Two failures in the same mirror set
    → not survivable
```
