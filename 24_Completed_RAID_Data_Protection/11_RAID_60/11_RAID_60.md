# RAID 60

## 1. Overview

RAID 60 combines:

* RAID 6
* RAID 0

Its fundamental design is:

```text
Multiple RAID 6 Groups
          ↓
       RAID 0
          ↓
       RAID 60
```

The purpose of RAID 60 is to combine:

```text
RAID 6
→ Dual-parity data protection

RAID 0
→ Striping across multiple RAID 6 groups
```

The resulting architecture provides:

```text
Dual-parity protection
+
Distributed storage
+
Parallel I/O across multiple RAID 6 groups
```

RAID 60 does not create one large RAID 6 protection domain.

Instead, multiple independent RAID 6 groups are created first and then
striped together using RAID 0.

---

# 2. Why RAID 60 Exists

A single RAID 6 group provides:

```text
Striping
+
Dual parity
+
Two-member fault tolerance
```

However, large RAID 6 configurations may create:

```text
Large protection domains
+
Large rebuild scopes
+
Large numbers of members participating in recovery
```

RAID 60 divides the physical members into smaller RAID 6 groups.

Conceptually:

```text
RAID 6 Group 1
        +
RAID 6 Group 2
        +
RAID 6 Group 3
        ↓
     RAID 0
```

This creates a striped set of independently protected RAID 6 groups.

---

# 3. RAID 60 Architecture

The general RAID 60 architecture is:

```text
                         RAID 60
                            |
                         RAID 0
                     /      |      \
                    /       |       \
                 RAID 6   RAID 6   RAID 6
                 Group 1  Group 2  Group 3
```

For the laboratory configuration, two RAID 6 component arrays were used:

```text
                  md60
                 RAID 0
                /      \
              md61     md62
             RAID 6    RAID 6
            / |  \ \   / |  \ \
```

The actual four-member component layout was:

```text
md61:
loop10
loop11
loop12
loop13

md62:
loop14
loop15
loop16
loop17
```

Therefore:

```text
md60 → top-level RAID 0
md61 → component RAID 6
md62 → component RAID 6
```

---

# 4. RAID 60 Data Protection Model

Each RAID 6 component provides:

```text
Data
+
P parity
+
Q parity
```

The important distinction from RAID 5 is that RAID 6 maintains two independent
parity relationships.

Conceptually:

```text
RAID 6
   |
   +── P parity
   |
   +── Q parity
```

This allows the RAID 6 component to normally tolerate:

```text
Two failed members
```

within the same RAID 6 protection domain.

RAID 60 then stripes across multiple such protected domains.

---

# 5. The Most Important RAID 60 Rule

The most important RAID 60 engineering rule is:

> **Each RAID 6 component must remain operational.**

A RAID 6 component can normally tolerate:

```text
0 failures → healthy
1 failure  → degraded but operational
2 failures → degraded but operational
```

But:

```text
3 failures
```

within the same RAID 6 component exceed its normal two-member protection.

Therefore:

```text
RAID 6 component fails
        ↓
RAID 0 loses one required component
        ↓
RAID 60 becomes unavailable
```

Failure distribution across the RAID 6 groups is therefore critical.

---

# 6. RAID 60 Redundancy Mechanism

RAID 60 uses two layers with different responsibilities:

```text
RAID 6
→ Dual-parity protection

RAID 0
→ Striping
```

Conceptually:

```text
Physical members
      ↓
RAID 6 groups
      ↓
Data + P + Q
      ↓
RAID 0 striping
      ↓
Logical RAID 60
```

The RAID 0 layer does not provide redundancy.

All redundancy comes from the underlying RAID 6 groups.

---

# 7. P and Q Parity

RAID 6 maintains two independent parity calculations.

Conceptually:

```text
P parity
→ XOR-based parity relationship

Q parity
→ Independent second parity relationship
```

The exact Q calculation is more complex than simple XOR and uses a separate
coding relationship.

The important engineering principle is:

```text
P + Q
→ two independent parity relationships
→ two-member fault tolerance
```

RAID 60 inherits this dual-parity protection independently inside each RAID 6
component.

---

# 8. RAID 60 Has No Global Parity Domain

This is one of the most important concepts.

RAID 60 is not equivalent to:

```text
One very large RAID 6
```

Instead:

```text
RAID 6 Group 1
        +
RAID 6 Group 2
        +
RAID 6 Group 3
        ↓
RAID 0
```

Therefore:

```text
md61 parity
```

does not reconstruct:

```text
md62 data
```

and:

```text
md62 parity
```

does not reconstruct:

```text
md61 data
```

Every RAID 6 component has its own independent data and parity protection.

---

# 9. RAID 0 Layer

The top-level RAID 0 layer:

```text
md60
```

stripes logical data across the component RAID 6 arrays.

Conceptually:

```text
Logical data:

A B C D E F G H
```

may be distributed across:

```text
md61 → A C E G
md62 → B D F H
```

The exact mapping depends on the implementation and RAID geometry.

The critical principle is:

```text
md60
→ distributes logical data across RAID 6 components
```

It does not provide redundancy.

---

# 10. RAID 60 Read Path

A logical read enters the top-level RAID 0 layer.

Conceptually:

```text
Host
 ↓
RAID 60
 ↓
RAID 0
 ↓
Target RAID 6 component
 ↓
Available RAID 6 members
 ↓
Requested data
```

For a healthy array, normal reads do not require parity reconstruction merely
to access existing data.

When a member is degraded, RAID 6 can reconstruct unavailable information
using the surviving members and parity.

---

# 11. RAID 60 Write Path

A logical write first reaches the RAID 0 layer.

The target component RAID 6 then handles data and parity updates.

Conceptually:

```text
Host
 ↓
RAID 60
 ↓
RAID 0
 ↓
Selected RAID 6 group
 ↓
Data update
+
P parity maintenance
+
Q parity maintenance
 ↓
Physical members
```

The top-level RAID 0 layer does not calculate P or Q parity.

The component RAID 6 performs the required parity work.

---

# 12. RAID 60 Small-Write Behavior

RAID 60 inherits RAID 6 parity-maintenance behavior.

A partial write can involve:

```text
New data
+
P parity update
+
Q parity update
```

The exact implementation depends on the RAID engine and workload, but the
important engineering point is:

```text
RAID 60
→ retains RAID 6 parity-maintenance overhead
```

It does not behave like parity-free RAID 10.

---

# 13. RAID 60 Capacity

For equal-sized members, a simplified capacity model is:

```text
Usable Capacity
≈
Number of RAID 6 groups
×
(Group members - 2)
×
Member size
```

For the laboratory configuration:

```text
2 RAID 6 groups
4 members per group
1 GiB members
```

Each RAID 6 group contributes approximately:

```text
(4 - 2) × 1 GiB
=
2 GiB
```

Two groups therefore provide approximately:

```text
2 GiB + 2 GiB
=
4 GiB
```

The laboratory top-level RAID 60 reported approximately:

```text
3.99 GiB
```

because of RAID metadata and storage geometry.

---

# 14. Capacity Efficiency

For each RAID 6 component:

```text
Usable capacity
≈
(N - 2) × member size
```

For the four-member groups used in the laboratory:

```text
4 members
-
2 parity member-equivalents
=
2 usable member-equivalents
```

Therefore:

```text
2 usable
/
4 raw
=
50%
```

Ignoring metadata and alignment overhead, the six-, eight-, or larger-member
configuration's efficiency depends on the size of each RAID 6 component.

---

# 15. Minimum Member Count

A RAID 60 design requires multiple RAID 6 groups.

Each RAID 6 group requires enough members to support dual parity.

For the laboratory design:

```text
2 RAID 6 groups
×
4 members per group
=
8 members
```

Conceptually:

```text
Group 1 → D1 D2 D3 D4
Group 2 → D5 D6 D7 D8
```

Therefore the exact minimum depends on the implementation and chosen
component-group geometry, but the practical engineering requirement is:

```text
Multiple RAID 6 groups
```

with each group large enough for RAID 6.

---

# 16. Single-Member Failure

Suppose:

```text
md61:
D1 → FAILED
D2 → HEALTHY
D3 → HEALTHY
D4 → HEALTHY
```

Then:

```text
md61 → degraded
```

but RAID 6 retains one remaining parity level beyond the immediate
single-member loss.

Therefore:

```text
md61 → operational
```

If:

```text
md62 → healthy
```

then:

```text
md60 → operational
```

and the RAID 60 filesystem can remain accessible.

---

# 17. Two-Member Failure in One RAID 6 Group

Suppose:

```text
md61:
D1 → FAILED
D2 → FAILED
D3 → HEALTHY
D4 → HEALTHY
```

Then:

```text
md61 → two members failed
```

RAID 6 can normally tolerate this condition.

Conceptually:

```text
md61 → [__UU]
```

The component remains operational because RAID 6 provides:

```text
P + Q
```

dual-parity protection.

If:

```text
md62 → healthy
```

then:

```text
md60 → operational
```

and data remains accessible.

This is one of the most important differences between RAID 60 and RAID 50.

---

# 18. One Failure in Each RAID 6 Group

Consider:

```text
md61 → [_UUU]
md62 → [_UUU]
```

Each component has one failed member.

Both components remain operational.

Therefore:

```text
md60 → operational
RAID 60 → accessible
```

The failures are distributed across independent RAID 6 protection domains.

---

# 19. Two Failures in Each RAID 6 Group

This is the maximum failure scenario for the laboratory topology.

Consider:

```text
md61 → [__UU]
md62 → [__UU]
```

Each RAID 6 component has:

```text
2 failed
2 working
```

Both components are still within the normal RAID 6 two-member fault-tolerance
boundary.

Therefore:

```text
md61 → operational
md62 → operational
md60 → operational
```

The RAID 60 filesystem can remain accessible.

For the laboratory configuration:

```text
2 failures in md61
+
2 failures in md62
=
4 simultaneous failed members
```

This is a key RAID 60 validation scenario.

---

# 20. Why Four Failed Members Can Be Survivable

The statement:

```text
"RAID 60 can tolerate four disk failures"
```

must be qualified.

It is not a global unrestricted rule.

For the laboratory's two-component RAID 6 configuration:

```text
md61 → maximum 2 failures
md62 → maximum 2 failures
```

Therefore:

```text
2 + 2
```

is survivable.

But:

```text
3 + 1
```

is not survivable because the component with three failures has exceeded
its RAID 6 protection boundary.

Likewise:

```text
4 + 0
```

is not survivable.

Therefore:

> **RAID 60 fault tolerance depends on how failures are distributed across the component RAID 6 groups.**

---

# 21. RAID 60 Failure Matrix

For the laboratory's two RAID 6 groups:

| Failure Pattern | md61             | md62             | RAID 60     |
| --------------- | ---------------- | ---------------- | ----------- |
| 0 + 0           | Healthy          | Healthy          | Operational |
| 1 + 0           | Degraded         | Healthy          | Operational |
| 0 + 1           | Healthy          | Degraded         | Operational |
| 1 + 1           | Degraded         | Degraded         | Operational |
| 2 + 0           | Degraded         | Healthy          | Operational |
| 0 + 2           | Healthy          | Degraded         | Operational |
| 2 + 1           | Degraded         | Degraded         | Operational |
| 1 + 2           | Degraded         | Degraded         | Operational |
| 2 + 2           | Degraded         | Degraded         | Operational |
| 3 + 0           | Component failed | Healthy          | Unavailable |
| 0 + 3           | Healthy          | Component failed | Unavailable |
| 3 + 1           | Component failed | Degraded         | Unavailable |
| 1 + 3           | Degraded         | Component failed | Unavailable |

The central rule is:

```text
Every RAID 6 component must remain operational.
```

---

# 22. Three-Member Failure in One Component

Suppose:

```text
md61 → [___U]
md62 → [UUUU]
```

The RAID 6 component `md61` has lost three members.

That exceeds the normal two-member protection boundary.

Therefore:

```text
md61 → unavailable
```

The top-level RAID 0 requires `md61`.

Thus:

```text
md61 unavailable
      ↓
md60 loses required component
      ↓
RAID 60 unavailable
```

The healthy `md62` cannot reconstruct `md61`.

---

# 23. Failure Count vs Failure Distribution

RAID 60 troubleshooting must consider:

```text
Failure count
+
Failure distribution
```

For example:

```text
4 failed members
```

could mean:

```text
2 in md61
+
2 in md62
```

which is survivable for the laboratory topology.

But:

```text
3 in md61
+
1 in md62
```

is not survivable because `md61` exceeds its RAID 6 protection.

Therefore:

> **The total number of failed drives is not sufficient to determine RAID 60 survivability.**

---

# 24. RAID 60 Rebuild

When a RAID 6 member fails:

```text
Failed member
      ↓
Replacement member
      ↓
RAID 6 reconstruction
      ↓
P/Q-based recovery
      ↓
Write recovered data
      ↓
Component RAID 6 restored
```

The rebuild occurs inside the affected component:

```text
md61
```

or:

```text
md62
```

The top-level:

```text
md60
```

does not independently rebuild the physical member.

---

# 25. RAID 60 Reconstruction

RAID 6 provides two independent parity relationships:

```text
P
+
Q
```

This allows reconstruction when up to two members are unavailable within
the same RAID 6 component.

Conceptually:

```text
Surviving data
+
P parity
+
Q parity
      ↓
Missing information
      ↓
Replacement member
```

The actual reconstruction mathematics is more complex than the simplified
conceptual model.

The important engineering principle is:

```text
Two independent parity relationships
→ two-member recovery capability
```

---

# 26. Rebuild Workload

During RAID 60 rebuild:

```text
Normal application I/O
        +
Reads from surviving RAID 6 members
        +
P/Q reconstruction
        +
Writes to replacement
```

This can cause:

```text
Higher latency
Lower throughput
Additional storage activity
Longer recovery time
```

The exact impact depends on workload, storage hardware, RAID implementation,
and rebuild policy.

---

# 27. Rebuild Window

After a member failure:

```text
Healthy
   ↓
Member failure
   ↓
Degraded RAID 6
   ↓
Replacement added
   ↓
Recovery
   ↓
Healthy RAID 6
```

During recovery, the affected RAID 6 component has reduced redundancy.

If it has:

```text
1 failure
```

one additional member can still be tolerated.

If it already has:

```text
2 failures
```

the component is at its normal protection boundary.

Therefore rebuild management becomes especially important at the two-member
degraded state.

---

# 28. Failure During Rebuild

Suppose:

```text
md61 → [UU_U]
```

and a replacement is rebuilding.

If another member fails:

```text
md61 → [U__U]
```

the component may still be operating if the total unavailable members remain
within RAID 6's two-member fault-tolerance boundary.

However, if the component reaches:

```text
3 failed members
```

then:

```text
md61 → unavailable
```

and:

```text
md60 → loses md61
```

Therefore:

```text
RAID 6 protection boundary
+
Current rebuild state
```

must always be considered together.

---

# 29. Maximum Failure Scenario

For the laboratory topology:

```text
md61 → RAID 6 → 4 members
md62 → RAID 6 → 4 members
```

the maximum simultaneous member failure pattern that remains within the normal
protection boundary is:

```text
md61 → 2 failed
md62 → 2 failed
```

Therefore:

```text
Total failed members = 4
```

while:

```text
Each component still has 2 working members
```

The top-level RAID 0 remains operational as long as both component arrays
remain available.

---

# 30. RAID 60 vs RAID 50

RAID 50:

```text
RAID 5
+
RAID 0
```

RAID 60:

```text
RAID 6
+
RAID 0
```

Their component protection differs:

```text
RAID 50:
1 failure per RAID 5 component

RAID 60:
2 failures per RAID 6 component
```

For the same two-group structure:

```text
RAID 50:
1 + 1 → survivable
2 + 0 → not survivable

RAID 60:
2 + 2 → survivable
3 + 1 → not survivable
```

The key difference is the protection boundary of the component array.

---

# 31. RAID 60 vs RAID 6

RAID 6:

```text
One RAID 6 protection domain
```

RAID 60:

```text
Multiple RAID 6 protection domains
+
RAID 0 striping
```

RAID 60 therefore changes the failure-domain organization.

A two-member failure in one component remains within that component's RAID 6
protection boundary.

A third failure in that same component exceeds the boundary even if other
components remain healthy.

---

# 32. RAID 60 vs RAID 10

RAID 10 uses:

```text
Mirroring
+
Striping
```

RAID 60 uses:

```text
Dual parity
+
Striping
```

Therefore:

| Feature                 | RAID 10                        | RAID 60                                             |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Redundancy              | Mirroring                      | P + Q                                               |
| Parity                  | No                             | Yes                                                 |
| Striping                | Yes                            | Yes                                                 |
| Two-member failure      | Depends on mirror placement    | Normally tolerated within one RAID 6 component      |
| Four-member failure     | Depends on exact mirror layout | Can be survivable as 2 + 2 in the two-group example |
| Rebuild                 | Mirror copy                    | P/Q reconstruction                                  |
| Small-write parity work | No                             | Yes                                                 |

---

# 33. RAID 60 Degraded-State Validation

After failure, verify:

```bash
cat /proc/mdstat
```

Then inspect the affected component:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md61
```

or:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md62
```

Then inspect the top-level array:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md60
```

Important fields include:

```text
State
Active Devices
Working Devices
Failed Devices
Spare Devices
Raid Devices
Raid Level
Layout
Chunk Size
```

---

# 34. Data Accessibility Validation

After confirming the RAID state, validate filesystem access.

Check:

```bash
mount | grep /mnt/raid60
```

Then:

```bash
ls -lh /mnt/raid60
```

Read known test data:

```bash
cat /mnt/raid60/testfile_01.txt
```

The validation chain is:

```text
RAID degraded
      ↓
Component still operational
      ↓
md60 still active
      ↓
Filesystem mounted
      ↓
Files readable
```

---

# 35. Data Integrity Validation

Do not depend only on file readability.

Create a checksum baseline:

```bash
sha256sum /mnt/raid60/testfile_01.txt \
          /mnt/raid60/testfile_02.txt \
          /mnt/raid60/testfile_03.txt
```

Repeat after:

```text
Failure
Rebuild
Final recovery
```

The laboratory baseline was:

```text
testfile_01.txt
ef8b994b4eaa76583e14ada0b7b18aed7e5623a831ebd549f09745f64ca2255c

testfile_02.txt
4ba4b039185bc6fe0f670e848c1c2796d2a35741dca74f576c8179e625050689

testfile_03.txt
2923e5735562db666ae0ea364096db080e9f35957d4b07422d71f739a009f588
```

Matching hashes provide evidence that the test data remained unchanged.

# 36. Replacement Member Validation

Before adding a replacement:

```bash
sudo mdadm --examine /dev/loopX
```

A clean replacement should report:

```text
mdadm: No md superblock detected on /dev/loopX.
```

Then add it to the correct component:

```bash
sudo mdadm --manage /dev/md61 --add /dev/loopX
```

or:

```bash
sudo mdadm --manage /dev/md62 --add /dev/loopX
```

The target component must always be verified before adding the replacement.

---

# 37. Rebuild Monitoring

Monitor:

```bash
cat /proc/mdstat
```

or:

```bash
watch -n 2 cat /proc/mdstat
```

Look for:

```text
recovery = XX.X%
```

and eventually:

```text
[UUUU]
```

for the affected RAID 6 component.

For the two-component laboratory topology:

```text
md61 → [UUUU]
md62 → [UUUU]
```

represents the fully restored component state.

---

# 38. Rebuild Completion

Do not assume rebuild completion merely because the replacement was added.

Verify:

```bash
cat /proc/mdstat
```

Then:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md61
```

and:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md62
```

Expected healthy component state:

```text
State             : clean
Active Devices    : expected count
Working Devices   : expected count
Failed Devices    : 0
```

Then inspect:

```bash
sudo mdadm --detail /dev/md60
```

Finally verify data integrity.

# 39. Filesystem Troubleshooting

If RAID appears healthy but the filesystem is inaccessible, troubleshoot
the layers separately.

```text
Physical member
      ↓
RAID 6 component
      ↓
RAID 0 layer
      ↓
/dev/md60
      ↓
Filesystem
      ↓
Mount
      ↓
Application
```

Useful commands:

```bash
lsblk -f
```

```bash
mount | grep /mnt/raid60
```

```bash
df -h /mnt/raid60
```

Do not automatically classify filesystem problems as RAID problems.

Possible higher-layer problems include:

```text
Filesystem corruption
Mount failure
Permission problem
Application problem
```

# 42. RAID 60 Failure-Domain Model

The central hierarchy is:

```text
                       RAID 60
                          |
                       RAID 0
                     /       \
                    ↓         ↓
                 RAID 6     RAID 6
                  md61       md62
                 / | \ \     / | \ \
                D  D  D  D  D  D  D  D
```

When a member fails:

```text
Failed member
      ↓
Identify RAID 6 component
      ↓
Count failures in that component
      ↓
Is component still operational?
```

If yes:

```text
Component survives
      ↓
md60 can remain operational
      ↓
RAID 60 remains accessible
```

If no:

```text
Component RAID 6 fails
      ↓
md60 loses required RAID 0 component
      ↓
RAID 60 becomes unavailable
```

# 43. RAID 60 Maximum Failure Model

For the laboratory's two four-member RAID 6 components:

```text
md61 → maximum 2 failed
md62 → maximum 2 failed
```

Therefore:

```text
2 + 2
```

is within both component protection boundaries.

This produces:

```text
4 simultaneous failed members
```

while retaining:

```text
2 working members in md61
+
2 working members in md62
```

The top-level RAID 0 remains operational because both RAID 6 components remain
available.

But:

```text
3 + 1
```

is not survivable because one component exceeds its RAID 6 protection
boundary.

# 45. RAID 60 Engineering Comparison

| Feature                         | RAID 50                   | RAID 60                    |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| Component                       | RAID 5                    | RAID 6                     |
| Top-level layer                 | RAID 0                    | RAID 0                     |
| Component parity                | Single parity             | Dual parity                |
| Per-component failure tolerance | 1                         | 2                          |
| Same-component two failures     | Not normally tolerated    | Normally tolerated         |
| Same-component three failures   | Not normally tolerated    | Not normally tolerated     |
| Distributed protection          | One failure per component | Two failures per component |
| Rebuild                         | RAID 5 reconstruction     | RAID 6 P/Q reconstruction  |
| Small-write parity work         | Single parity maintenance | P + Q maintenance          |
| Capacity efficiency             | Higher                    | Lower due to dual parity   |

# 47. Final RAID 60 Mental Model

Think of RAID 60 as:

```text

                     /       \
                    ↓         ↓
                 RAID 6     RAID 6

                [UUUU]      [UUUU]
```

# 48. Key Engineering Takeaways

1. RAID 60 combines multiple RAID 6 groups with RAID 0 striping.
2. RAID 6 provides dual-parity protection inside each component.
3. The top-level RAID 0 provides striping but no redundancy.
4. There is no global parity relationship between RAID 6 component groups.
5. Each RAID 6 component is an independent failure domain.
6. One failed member in a component is normally survivable.
7. Two failed members in a component are normally survivable.
8. Two failures in each of two RAID 6 components can therefore be survivable.
9. The laboratory's 2+2 pattern demonstrated four simultaneous failed members while the RAID 60 remained accessible.
10. Three failures inside the same RAID 6 component exceed its normal protection boundary.
11. A failed RAID 6 component causes the top-level RAID 0 to lose a required component.
12. Rebuilds occur inside the affected RAID 6 component.
13. RAID 60 rebuilds involve dual-parity reconstruction.
14. Rebuilds generate additional storage I/O.
15. Failure distribution is more important than total failure count alone.
16. Filesystem access must be validated separately from RAID metadata.
17. SHA-256 checksums provide stronger data-integrity evidence than simple file reads.
18. Replacement members should be checked for stale RAID metadata before being added.
19. Physical disk identity must be confirmed before replacing real hardware.
20. RAID 60 provides stronger component-level protection than RAID 50 at the cost of additional parity overhead and lower capacity efficiency.

# 49. Final Engineering Principle

> **Always evaluate RAID 60 from the RAID 6 component level upward.**

Use this reasoning:

```text
Failed member
      ↓
Identify RAID 6 component
      ↓
Count failures in that component
      ↓
Is the component within its two-member protection boundary?
      ↓
YES
 ↓
Check md60
 ↓
Check filesystem
 ↓
Check data integrity
 ↓
Rebuild
 ↓
Verify redundancy

NO
 ↓
RAID 6 component unavailable
 ↓
RAID 0 loses required component
 ↓
RAID 60 unavailable
```

For a two-component RAID 60:

```text
2 failures in md61
+
2 failures in md62
→ within component protection boundaries
→ RAID 60 can remain operational
```

but:

```text
3 failures in md61
+
any condition in md62
→ md61 protection boundary exceeded
→ RAID 60 unavailable
```

The central RAID 60 troubleshooting model is therefore:

> **Physical member → RAID 6 protection domain → RAID 0 aggregation → filesystem → application data.**
